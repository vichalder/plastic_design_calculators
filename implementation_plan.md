### 1. Viscoelasticity and Creep Mechanics

The fundamental nature of polymeric materials dictates that their structural response is entirely dependent on both time and temperature. Under a constant applied load, a plastic component will experience a continuous increase in strain, known as creep. Conversely, under a constant deformation, the internal stress within the plastic will decay logarithmically over time, known as stress relaxation. The computational agent must handle discrete load stepping, continuous load histories, and environmental temperature shifts through advanced superposition models.

The primary mathematical engine for determining the temporal strain response is the Boltzmann Superposition Principle. This principle states that the total strain response of a linear viscoelastic material to a complex stress history is mathematically equivalent to the sum of the individual strains resulting from each distinct load step applied independently. When a stress history consists of a sequence of discrete stress changes at specific intervals, the agent must implement the summation formula to calculate the total strain at time $t$. The equation is defined as:

$$\epsilon(t) = \sum_{i} \Delta\sigma_i J(t - t_i)$$

In this formulation, $\Delta\sigma_i$ represents the discrete change in stress applied at time $t_i$, and $J(t - t_i)$ represents the time-dependent creep compliance function of the material evaluated over the elapsed time since the load was applied. For scenarios involving continuous dynamic loading, the agent must transition from the discrete summation to the integral form of the Boltzmann Superposition Principle. This requires evaluating the convolution integral of the compliance function and the rate of stress application:

$$\epsilon(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} J(t - \tau) \frac{d\sigma(\tau)}{d\tau} d\tau$$

If the loading initiates from a zero-stress state at time $t=0$ with an initial instantaneous stress step $\sigma_0$, the agent must structure the algorithm to capture both the immediate elastic response and the subsequent continuous accumulation:

$$\epsilon(t) = \sigma_{0} J(t) + \int_{0}^{t} J(t - \tau) \frac{d\sigma(\tau)}{d\tau} d\tau$$

Because physical testing of polymers over a 20-year lifespan is impractical, the agent must utilize the principle of Time-Temperature Superposition (TTS) to extrapolate long-term material behavior from short-term data gathered at elevated temperatures. The mathematical vehicle for this translation is the Williams-Landel-Ferry (WLF) equation, which calculates the horizontal shift factor $a_T$. The agent must implement the WLF equation to map experimental data to the actual service conditions:

$$\log(a_T) = \frac{-C_1(T - T_{ref})}{C_2 + (T - T_{ref})}$$

Here, $T$ denotes the actual operating temperature of the application, $T_{ref}$ is the reference temperature of the baseline material data, and $C_1$ and $C_2$ are empirically derived material constants. The agent must incorporate established constants for common polymers; for example, the constants for certain polycarbonate formulations at a reference temperature of $145^\circ\text{C}$ are documented as $C_1 = 22.87$ and $C_2 = 78.09$.

When evaluating components subjected to fluctuating environmental temperatures over their service life, the agent must compute a total effective reduced time. The actual time increments spent at various temperatures are mathematically shifted to equivalent durations at the reference temperature using the ratio of shift factors. The reduced time equation the agent must code is:

$$t_{effective} = \sum \left( t_i \cdot \frac{\alpha_{T_{ref}}}{\alpha_{T_i}} \right)$$

For example, the shift factor values for a specific Polypropylene (PP) material evaluated against a $23^\circ\text{C}$ reference temperature yield $\alpha_{23} = 45306.05$, $\alpha_{40} = 80.84544$, and $\alpha_{60} = 0.674462$. The agent must use these specific shift factors to normalize thermal histories. To calculate residual strain after a complex load and temperature history followed by a period of unloaded recovery, the agent must execute a dual-compliance calculation:

$$\epsilon = \sigma \cdot [J(t_{total\_effective})]_{23} - \sigma \cdot [J(t_{recovery})]_{23}$$

In the logical sequence for the Jupyter Notebook, the agent will begin by initializing the stress history arrays and the corresponding temperature profiles. The code must then dynamically calculate the continuous array of shift factors using the WLF equation, seamlessly generating the reduced time vector. Following this, a vectorized `numpy` routine must execute the Boltzmann superposition integral, predicting the exact dimensional deformation of the plastic component at the end of its intended lifecycle.

### 2. Integral Hinges (Living Hinges)

Integral hinges, commonly referred to as living hinges, represent one of the most mechanically demanding applications of plastic engineering. These structures rely on the extreme elongation capabilities and highly oriented molecular chains of semi-crystalline polymers, predominantly Polypropylene (PP) and Polyethylene (PE), to endure immense flexural fatigue without failure. When the hinge is bent for the first time immediately after molding, the polymer chains undergo cold drawing, orienting themselves along the axis of strain and massively increasing the localized tensile strength.

To ensure the structural integrity and infinite fatigue life of an integral hinge, the geometric relationship between the hinge length and the bend radius is paramount. The computational agent must design the hinge such that it forms a perfect, stress-distributing semicircle upon reaching its fully closed state. The analytical model for the optimal length of a hinge designed to bend through a specific flex angle $\theta$ is defined by the following parametric equation:

$$L = \left(\frac{\theta}{180}\right) \pi R$$

where $L$ is the developed length of the hinge web, $\theta$ is the flex angle in degrees, and $R$ is the bend radius, measured from the mating surface of the rigid body to the center axis of the hinge web. For the most common application of a standard 180-degree closure, the formula elegantly simplifies to $L = \pi R$.

The thickness of the hinge web is equally critical. If the hinge is too thin, it will impede the flow of molten plastic during the injection molding process, leading to premature freeze-off or structural tearing. Conversely, if the web is too thick, the bending action will induce excessive outer-fiber strain, leading to rapid fatigue failure. The agent must apply empirical thickness rules, typically setting the hinge thickness $T$ between $0.2 \text{ mm}$ and $0.5 \text{ mm}$, or dynamically calculating it based on the thickness of the adjoining rigid wall $H$. The formula for dynamic thickness scaling is:

$$T = \frac{H}{5} \text{ to } \frac{H}{8}$$

In addition to dimensional optimization, the notebook must conduct rigorous fatigue life forecasting. Living hinges are generally required to survive between $1,000,000$ and $10,000,000$ flexural cycles. The computational agent must calculate the peak bending moment $M_b$ and the resulting maximum outer-fiber strain during the flexure event. This deformation amplitude must be continuously cross-referenced against established fatigue data curves for the specific polymer, such as Hostalen PPR 1042. Because the cyclic loading of a living hinge frequently pushes the material well beyond its elastic limit, the agent must incorporate low-cycle fatigue models that account for plastic strain accumulation. The total strain amplitude must be computed by summing the elastic and plastic strain components using the combined Basquin and Coffin-Manson formulas:

$$\epsilon_a = \frac{\sigma'_f}{E}(2N_f)^b + \epsilon'_f(2N_f)^c$$

where $N_f$ represents the number of cycles to failure, and the coefficients define the material's specific fatigue ductility and strength properties. The generated code must utilize `scipy.optimize` to solve this nonlinear equation, iterating the geometric parameters until the projected cycle life safely exceeds the target specification.

### 3. Cantilever Snap-Fit Dynamics

Cantilever snap-fits are the most ubiquitous form of assembly architecture in modern plastic design. The mechanics of a cantilever snap-fit involve deflecting a flexural beam to bypass an interference point, allowing a locking undercut to engage. The computational agent must generate models that balance two competing physics: the beam must possess enough flexibility to deflect without exceeding the material's yield strain, yet it must retain sufficient stiffness to resist the separation forces encountered during product use.

The foundational mathematical model relies on an adaptation of classical beam theory specifically tailored for large deflections in thermoplastics. The maximum strain $\epsilon_{max}$ occurs at the geometric root of the cantilever beam and is calculated using the equation:

$$\epsilon_{max} = \frac{1.5 \cdot t \cdot Y}{L^2 \cdot Q}$$

where $t$ represents the thickness of the beam at the root, $Y$ denotes the maximum deflection distance required to clear the undercut, $L$ is the effective length of the beam, and $Q$ serves as a deflection-magnification factor that accounts for the compliance of the wall to which the beam is attached. For rigid walls, the agent must set $Q = 1$. The computational agent must rigorously check this calculated strain against the maximum permissible strain limits of the material. For repeated assembly and disassembly cycles, the allowable strain is aggressively de-rated to prevent localized yielding. The agent must code the specific limit check:

$$\epsilon_{allowable} = 0.6 \cdot 0.7 \cdot \epsilon_{yield}$$

For geometries assuming a half-circular cross-section, the deflection limit is modified. The agent must implement the adapted relationship: $Y_{max} = 0.578 \cdot \epsilon \cdot \frac{L^2}{r}$, allowing the calculation of engaging clearances.

Once the geometric strain limits are satisfied, the notebook must calculate the assembly and disassembly forces. The theoretical perpendicular deflection force $P$ required to bend the beam is derived from its flexural stiffness:

$$P = \frac{b \cdot t^2 \cdot E \cdot \epsilon_{max}}{6 \cdot L}$$

where $b$ is the width of the beam and $E$ is the material's secant flexural modulus evaluated at the specified strain rate. However, this perpendicular force does not represent the actual insertion effort. During physical assembly, the mating parts slide against an angled lead-in surface, introducing severe frictional resistance. The agent must map the perpendicular force through the friction cone utilizing the lead angle $\alpha$ and the dynamic coefficient of friction $\mu$. The true mating force $W$ is expressed as:

$$W = P \cdot \left[ \frac{\mu + \tan\alpha}{1 - \mu \tan\alpha} \right]$$

This equation is paramount for understanding ergonomic assembly limits. An identical calculation must be programmed to determine the retention or pull-off force, substituting the lead angle $\alpha$ with the return angle $\beta$. The agent must implement a logical trigger: if the return angle approaches 90 degrees, the denominator approaches an asymptote, and the code must formally classify the joint as an inseparable, permanent snap-fit. Furthermore, the agent must embed markdown warnings instructing the user that classical beam theory begins to degrade under extreme deflection ratios, and that nonlinear finite element analysis should be consulted if the deflection exceeds 20% of the beam length.

### 4. Press Fit Tolerances and Stress Relaxation

Press fits, or interference fits, represent a method of joining a metal shaft to a plastic hub utilizing immense radial pressure generated by deliberately overlapping dimensional tolerances. Unlike metallic assemblies, which store potential energy in a perfectly elastic state indefinitely, polymeric hubs act as highly damped springs that continually dissipate energy. The radial pressure that holds the joint together on the first day of assembly will decay continuously over a 10 or 20-year lifespan due to stress relaxation. The computational agent must accurately predict this long-term torque decay to prevent catastrophic field failures.

The interference dimension $\delta$ is the absolute diametral overlap between the rigid metal shaft and the flexible plastic hub:

$$\delta = D_1 - D$$

where $D_1$ is the nominal outer diameter of the metal shaft and $D$ is the undisturbed inner diameter of the plastic hub. Because the stiffness of steel or brass is orders of magnitude greater than that of unreinforced plastic, the analytical model assumes the metal shaft is perfectly rigid, forcing all radial deformation to occur within the plastic hub. To determine the resulting contact pressure, the agent must implement the modified Lamé equations for thick-walled cylinders:

$$P(t) = \frac{\delta}{D_1} \cdot E_r(t) \cdot \frac{1}{A + \nu}$$

In this critical equation, $E_r(t)$ represents the time-dependent relaxation modulus of the specific polymer evaluated at the target lifespan, $\nu$ is the Poisson's ratio, and $A$ represents the geometry factor that mathematically captures the structural rigidity of the hub based on its outer-to-inner diameter ratio ($D_2/D_1$). For example, the agent must draw upon established data that for unreinforced Polyoxymethylene (POM) serving for 1000 hours, $E_r(t)$ decays to $1200 \text{ N/mm}^2$, whereas a 30% glass-filled variant retains a modulus of $5000 \text{ N/mm}^2$ over the same duration.

The primary function of the press fit is torque transmission. The maximum transmittable torque $M_t$ is determined by integrating the frictional shear force over the entire cylindrical contact area. The agent must implement the torque capacity formula:

$$M_t = \pi \cdot D_1 \cdot \frac{D_1}{2} \cdot L \cdot P(t) \cdot \mu$$

which simplifies analytically to $M_t = \frac{\pi}{2} D_1^2 L P(t) \mu$, where $L$ is the active engagement length of the hub and $\mu$ is the static coefficient of friction.

The logical sequence for the press fit computation engine requires the agent to accept the target operational lifespan and the maximum environmental temperature. The algorithm must automatically calculate the degraded relaxation modulus $E_r(t)$ for that precise time and temperature coordinate. It will then compute the geometry factor $A$, derive the residual contact pressure $P(t)$, and output the surviving torque capacity. The notebook must generate a logarithmic decay plot demonstrating how $M_t$ trends downward over decades of service, highlighting the exact chronological intersection where the holding capacity falls below the required operational torque.

### 5. Mould Design and Manufacturability Guidelines

A theoretically perfect parametric design is completely useless if it cannot be physically manufactured. The injection molding process dictates strict geometric constraints that must be adhered to to prevent component warpage, internal voids, and ejection failures. The computational agent must integrate a suite of Design for Manufacturability (DFM) rules directly into the core analysis tools, bridging the gap between structural performance and toolmaking reality.

As molten polymer is injected under high pressure and subsequently cools, it undergoes significant specific volume contraction according to its pressure-volume-temperature (pvT) relationship. The physical steel cavity of the mold must be machined to a larger dimension to compensate for this shrinkage. The agent must implement the exact shrinkage compensation formula to calculate the necessary tool dimension $M_d$:

$$M_d = \frac{P_d}{1 - S_r}$$

where $P_d$ is the final desired dimension of the plastic part at ambient temperature, and $S_r$ is the specific volumetric shrinkage rate of the chosen polymer (e.g., $0.015$ for a material exhibiting $1.5\%$ shrinkage).

Furthermore, to allow the rigid plastic part to be forcefully ejected from the steel core without sustaining severe frictional damage or locking the tool entirely, the agent must program strict validation checks for draft angles. A fundamental parametric rule requires a minimum of 1 degree of draft taper per 1 inch of cavity depth, with an absolute hard limit of $0.5^\circ$ on all vertical surfaces aligned with the primary axis of tool draw. Any geometric matrix containing a vertical face with exactly $0^\circ$ of draft must instantly trigger a critical manufacturing failure warning.

To prevent structural warpage and internal sink marks, the part design must maintain a rigorously uniform wall thickness. Variations in cross-sectional mass lead to differential cooling rates; thicker sections remain molten longer, pulling material away from thinner, frozen sections, thus warping the entire geometry. The agent must program the notebook to scan the coordinate thickness array of the part. If the variation $(t_{max} - t_{min}) / t_{max}$ exceeds $10\%$, the notebook must output a high-severity warning.

The agent must also encode parametric logic regarding tool layout. Gate positioning—the point where molten plastic enters the cavity—must always be located at the thickest cross-section of the part, allowing the melt to flow organically from thick regions into thin regions, ensuring adequate packing pressure before freeze-off. Additionally, ejector pin locations must be distributed symmetrically across structurally rigid features, such as the base of intersecting ribs or heavy bosses, to prevent the pins from punching through or permanently deforming the hot, compliant plastic during the demolding cycle.

### 6. Material Selection Protocol: The 5-Step Method

Selecting the optimal polymeric matrix requires navigating thousands of competing property variables. The computational agent must construct a structured decision engine based on the established "5-Step Method" for polymer selection. This algorithm utilizes a property translation matrix to systematically filter out incompatible materials and isolate ideal candidates.

1.  **List Product Demands:** The notebook must accept an array of qualitative and quantitative application requirements. For example, a complex kitchen implement may require FDA approval for food contact, resistance to continuous exposure at $150^\circ\text{C}$, and high-volume manufacturability.
2.  **Translate Demands to Component A:** The agent must translate the functional demands of the primary feature into quantifiable engineering metrics. For a component requiring high flexibility (such as the bowl of a specialized spoon or a living hinge), the translation matrix maps "flexibility" to a Flexural Modulus constraint (e.g., $< 1500 \text{ MPa}$) and an Elongation at Yield constraint (e.g., $> 15\%$).
3.  **Translate Demands to Component B:** For multi-material applications, the secondary feature must be evaluated independently. If the handle must bear significant structural loads without deflecting, the matrix translates "stiffness" to a Tensile Modulus constraint (e.g., $> 5000 \text{ MPa}$), immediately restricting the database to highly crystalline or glass-fiber reinforced polymers.
4.  **Suggest Material A Candidates:** The `pandas` engine executes a boolean masking operation against a comprehensive internal material database. It filters the matrix to isolate polymers satisfying the flexibility, thermal, and regulatory constraints simultaneously.
5.  **Suggest Material B Candidates:** The engine repeats the query for the high-stiffness constraints.
